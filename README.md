# Video Map

Video and a live, detailed 3D map of **Riyadh**, by day and by night, on one
screen. A web app for the phone: no App Store, no Apple developer account, no
Mac.

The app is made for Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and for now for Riyadh only: the
map opens on the city and keeps to it, and places are looked for in it.

## What it does

**Video**

- From the phone (Photos or Files), from a YouTube link, or from a direct link
  to a video file or stream (`.mp4`, `.m3u8`, …).

**Map**

- A real map of Riyadh, from Dir'iyah and the edge of the plateau in the west
  to beyond the airport in the north, with street and place names in Arabic
  and in Latin letters. It opens on Olaya, by Kingdom Centre, until it knows
  where you are; it cannot be moved off the city. Away from Riyadh the map
  stays on the city and says so; the speed still shows.
- **3D City**, the look it opens with: the city as an **isometric** scale
  model. It is seen corner-on from the classic isometric angle, through a lens
  so long that parallel streets stay parallel. **Every building stands on its
  own outline**, a house at a time, with grey roads at their real widths.
- **All of it drawn, in 3D. No satellite photo is shown.** The ground, the
  roofs, the walls and everything standing on them is modelled or drawn from
  map data. The satellite photo is only read, out of sight, for three things
  no map has: where the trees stand, how tall the buildings are, and what
  colour their roofs are.
- **Walls worked out point by point.** Every wall is set out in floors and
  bays, and each point of it is worked out as it is drawn: wall or glass, the
  frame round a window and the sill under it, the shade the window's head
  throws on the glass by how high the sun stands, a blind behind some panes.
  A house has fewer and smaller windows than a block of flats; a building the
  map calls commercial has shops along its foot, each under a signboard; a
  tower is glass in a frame of metal, lighter the higher it goes, with the
  sun glinting in it as the map turns. Every wall runs up past its roof as a
  parapet. From far off, where a window would be a speck, a wall is the even
  mixture of its wall and its glass, so nothing flickers.
- **Colours from the roofs.** No source says what colour a wall is, but a
  building is seldom far in colour from its own roof. The app reads each
  roof's colour in the satellite photo where the sun is on it, and gives the
  walls a calmer, lighter shade of it: sand under a sand-coloured roof, grey
  under a grey one, warm under red tiles. Where a wall colour is on record,
  that is used.
- **Sun, sky and shadow on every wall.** A wall that faces the sun is bright
  and warm, the warmer the lower the sun; one that faces away is lit by the
  sky alone; and every wall darkens towards its foot, where the street
  gathers shade.
- **Riyadh by night.** As the sun sets over the city the model goes dark,
  little by little, and its lights come on. The streets glow the way they do
  from the air: white along the great roads, the amber of sodium lamps on the
  side streets. One room in three has a lamp on, each a little different in
  colour and brightness; offices are white, and now and then a whole floor of
  a tower is lit. Shops are lit from end to end under their signboards, and
  their light falls on the pavement. The foot of every wall catches the light
  of the street lamps. Round the top of many a tower runs a band of light in
  a colour of its own, and its glass gives back the glow of the streets
  below. Up on some roofs a lamp is left on by the door at the head of the
  stairs, and under every awning where people sit of an evening. Playing
  fields lie under floodlights and swimming pools are lit from within. The
  names on the map are written light on dark.
- **Street lamps.** Along every street stand lamp posts, by day as well: tall
  ones with long arms along the great roads, in the median where the two
  carriageways are mapped apart; by turns on either side of a wide street;
  down one side of a residential street; on the parapets of bridges; and in
  car parks. By night each has a point of light at its head and throws a pool
  of light on the road. From across the city the lamps are dots of light
  along the streets.
- **Lit landmarks.** By night Kingdom Centre shows the band of light along
  its arch and its lit sky bridge, Al Faisaliah its glowing golden ball, the
  TV Tower its lantern, the KAFD station the light through its pierced shell.
  Mosques are floodlit, with green lamps on the balcony and cap of each
  minaret; metro stations and their footbridges glow gold; the bands and
  signs of chain stores and fuel stations are lit in their own colours. A red
  lamp stands on the mast of every tall tower.
- **Headlights.** By night every vehicle has two white lamps ahead, their
  beam on the road, and two red lamps behind; buses and trains have their
  windows lit.
- **Day or night whenever you like.** Under the map options the light can be
  left to the real sun over Riyadh ("Real time"), or the city can be shown by
  day or by night whatever the hour.
- **Buildings shade each other.** A tower's shadow does not stop at the next
  wall: it runs up it, across the roof, and on down the other side, on over
  the cars and trees in the street, in step with the shadows on the ground.
- **A roof on every building, modelled.** Each roof is cut along its
  building's outline and laid inside its parapet, in the colour its real roof
  has: sand, grey, white, the red of tiles. Low buildings are roofed with
  concrete slabs, their joints showing from close by; tall ones with gravel;
  works, sheds and stores with ribbed metal sheet. A parapet has thickness: a
  coping along its top and an inner side, and it throws a strip of shade on
  its own roof that grows as the sun sinks.
- **What stands on a roof.** On a house the room at the head of the stairs, a
  white water tank, the outdoor halves of its air conditioners in a row, on
  some an awning to sit under or a few solar panels. On a block more of the
  same; on a large roof rows of cooling plant; on a tower the lift's machine
  room, and a mast on the tallest. Each throws its shade across the roof.
- **Car parks full of cars.** Beside the lanes of a car park, and beside
  other service lanes that have the room, cars stand in rows of bays, nose
  or tail to the lane, with the bays of a proper car park marked out in
  white. Never in a building, on a road, on a pavement or against a tree.
- **Riyadh's landmarks, modelled by hand.** Kingdom Centre with its arch and
  sky bridge, Al Faisaliah with its golden globe, the PIF Tower, Burj Rafal,
  the twisting Al Majdoul Tower, the TV Tower, the striped water tower and
  the KAFD metro station each have their own shape, built from reference
  photos on the building's real outline, in place of a plain block.
- **Bridges you can drive over.** Flyovers, interchange ramps and metro
  viaducts are built in 3D: a deck with parapets on piers, with the traffic
  and the trains up on it. A bridge is as high as what it passes over: one
  that crosses nothing stands at the usual height of a flyover or a viaduct,
  and one that crosses another bridge stands a level above it, whatever level
  number the map gives it. The Wadi Laban Bridge on the Western Ring Road has
  its two masts and fans of cables.
- **Metro stations that look like stations.** A station building wears the
  golden screen and vaulted roof of the Riyadh Metro, with covered footbridges
  over the road to its entrances; an entrance to an underground station gets
  a glass pavilion with a sign.
- **Chain stores in their own colours.** A branch of a chain the app knows
  (some fifty: restaurants, cafés, supermarkets, fuel stations, banks, shops)
  wears that chain's colours: its own building painted and banded, a shop
  front under a coloured band where it shares a building, a canopy with pumps
  at a fuel station. Colours and shapes only; no logos or names are drawn.
- **A front for every shop.** Every other shop, restaurant, café, bank and
  pharmacy on the map gets a small shop front on the wall of its building
  that faces the street: glass, an awning coloured by what it sells, and a
  sign. Every other fuel station gets a plain canopy with pumps.
- **Mosques with domes and minarets.** Each mosque on the map has a dome on
  its roof and a minaret at a corner, two on a large one.
- **Pavements.** Along both sides of every street and main road runs a
  pavement of pale paving, with a kerb at the road and rounded corners at the
  junctions; a side street runs through the pavement of the road it meets.
- **Painted roads.** Main roads have their lanes: a broken line between lanes,
  a line along each edge, a yellow line down the middle where traffic runs
  both ways. A side street has a zebra crossing where it meets a main road,
  and so has every arm of a crossroads of main roads.
- **Traffic on the real streets.** Cars, vans and lorries drive the streets:
  they keep to the lanes, drive on the right, obey one-way streets, queue
  behind each other, turn at junctions, disappear into tunnels, and are busier
  in the rush hours than at night. Each stands in its own shade, which lies
  on the road from under it. Cars stand parked along the kerbs of residential
  streets. This is a simulation to bring the model to life, **not
  live traffic**: where each vehicle is, is made up. It can be switched off
  under the map options.
- **Buses where buses run.** A bus only drives along a street that has a bus
  stop on the map (and on along the same street between stops), in the lane by
  the kerb, and pulls in at every stop on its side of the street, where a
  shelter stands. About one bus every ten minutes each way, from 05:00 to
  midnight, and none at night.
- **The metro on its lines, by its timetable's rhythm.** Trains run on the
  metro tracks the map shows: every four minutes in the rush hours and every
  seven and a half otherwise, from 05:30 to midnight (from 10:00 on Fridays),
  keeping to the right-hand track and standing at each station. A train
  always has track under it: on a viaduct its deck and rails, on the ground a
  bed of ballast with the rails down its middle. Under ground a train is out
  of sight, and comes into view car by car at the tunnel's mouth. No trains
  are put on the main-line railway or on the monorail of the financial
  district. See the limits below for what this is and is not.
- **Trees where they really stand.** The app reads the satellite photo under
  the city for dark green crowns and stands a tree at each one, sized to its
  crown, with its shade on the ground: a tree with a round crown in bands of
  green, darker underneath, or a date palm. Never on a road, a track or a
  building.
- **Heights from shadows.** Few ordinary buildings have a height on record.
  The app looks up the day the satellite photo was taken, works out where the
  sun stood, measures each building's shadow in the photo and stands the
  building up to match. An estimate; see the limits below. The trees, these
  heights and the colours read from the photo can be switched off together
  under the map options, which also saves the download of the photo.
- **The ground in detail**, all of it drawn from the map data: bare plots the
  colour of sand and dust, lawns and gardens, dry scrub, woods, open sand,
  the grounds of schools and hospitals, the yards of works, the forecourts of
  shops, paved squares, playing fields and running tracks, ponds, the
  swimming pools in the gardens; and tunnels as a faint trace, metro and
  railway lines, footpaths.
- **Real sunlight.** The model is lit from where the sun actually is over
  Riyadh at that hour, and every building casts the shadow it casts right
  now: long in the morning and evening, short at noon, none after sunset,
  when the city goes over to its night (see above). Under cloud the shadows
  are fainter, and under a covered sky there are next to none.
- **A sky with depth.** Turned the way you travel, the view runs along the
  road to the horizon with the sky over the skyline, and what is in that sky
  is what is over Riyadh at that moment:
  - **The clouds the weather has**, in the three layers it tells of (low,
    middle and high), as much of each as it says there is. Each layer lies
    level at its own height, so it runs away to the horizon in ever thinner
    bands; each drifts on the wind, the higher the faster. By day they are
    white with grey bellies, heaped up when there are few of them and a flat
    sheet when the sky is covered; as the sun goes down the high ones stay
    lit longest and the low ones go dark first; by night the city's own
    light is on their undersides.
  - **The sun** where it stands, red as it goes down into the haze, with
    the glow along the horizon on its own side and, on the far side, the
    pink band over the grey-blue shadow of the Earth.
  - **The moon** where it really stands, with as much of its face lit as it
    has, the lit side towards the sun.
  - **The stars and the planets** where they really stand: the 483 stars to
    the fourth magnitude that rise over Riyadh, each in its own colour, and
    Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. They come out one by one as it
    darkens, the brightest first (Venus while the sky is still light), and
    the stars twinkle, the more the lower they stand.
- **Aircraft, by the airport's real rhythm.** Aircraft come in to King
  Khalid International Airport, climb out of it and pass high over the
  city, drawn larger than life or they would be specks. The high ones leave
  trails; by night each shows its lamps (red on the left wing, green on the
  right, white at the tail, the flashing ones) and, coming in, its landing
  lamps. If the open feed of live aircraft positions has any over Riyadh,
  those are the aircraft shown, each where it really is. It rarely has (see
  the limits below), and then the sky is flown as the airport really flies
  it: as many arrivals and departures an hour as it has at that hour of the
  day, on its two real runways, in the direction the real wind of the moment
  has them in use, down the real three-degree approach. Tap your own dot to
  see which of the two is being shown. Shown with the traffic; switched off
  with it.
- Drawing the walls window by window and working out the shadows on them is
  more work for the phone with every picture. If the map moves slowly, both
  can be switched off under the map options ("Detailed walls and shadows");
  the walls are then plain, with a simple pattern of windows, and the roofs
  stay. (On a computer that draws without a graphics chip, at a phone's
  sharpness, a picture takes 6 to 33 per cent longer with them. By night a
  picture takes about as long as by day, within a tenth either way: there are
  no shadows to work out then, and the lamps' light costs about as much. What
  any of it costs on a phone's own graphics chip has not been measured.)
- Four more looks: **Streets** (colours and icons), **Dark** (a flat map in
  dark colours), **Satellite** (real photos with the names on top) and
  **Detailed** (the classic OpenStreetMap picture). In the Satellite look the
  photo is fetched as sharp
  as the phone's screen can show it (in Riyadh down to about 30 cm to a
  pixel), with a coarse copy under it that is there at once; that
  takes several times the download, and can be switched off under the map
  options.
- Your position, direction of travel and speed. The round button switches
  between a view that does not turn (isometric in the 3D city, north-up in
  the other looks) and a **driving view** where the road ahead points up.
- The **name of the road** you are on, and its **speed limit** where
  OpenStreetMap has one. The speed turns red when you are over it.
- **Places** you choose to mark: fuel, charging, parking, food and coffee,
  mosques and other places of worship, health, banks and ATMs, shopping, hotels.
- **Tap any place** for what is known about it: opening hours, phone, website,
  address, fuel types, charging plugs, parking fee and more.
- **Search** Riyadh by name or address; nothing outside the city is looked
  for. **Press and hold** the map to drop a pin.
- **Route** to a place: the line on the map, the distance and the driving
  time. "Open in Maps" hands the place to the phone's own maps app for
  turn-by-turn directions.
- The **weather** where you are in the city (over the middle of Riyadh until
  that is known). Tap your own dot for your coordinates, altitude and the
  nearest address.

**Screen**

- One bar between video and map: show video, map or both, and drag it to
  resize. Upright the two are stacked; sideways they sit side by side.
- Keeps the screen on, remembers your choices, and opens without a connection
  once it has been opened before (the map and online video still need the
  internet).

## Where the information comes from

Everything is free and needs no account or key.

| What | From | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Street map, names, places, road names | [OpenFreeMap](https://openfreemap.org), built from [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) | Drawn on the phone by [MapLibre](https://maplibre.org) |
| Satellite photos | Esri World Imagery | © Esri, Maxar, Earthstar Geographics. Shown only in the Satellite look. How sharp the photo comes differs from place to place: the service is asked, a square of tiles at a time, and only what it has is fetched. In the 3D city the photo is never shown: it is read on the phone, at one middling level of sharpness, for trees, shadows and the colours of roofs, and the service is asked once per area for the day the photo was taken |
| One outline for every building in the 3D city | [Overture Maps](https://overturemaps.org) buildings, which join OpenStreetMap with outlines traced from satellite photos by Esri's community, Google and Microsoft | Read a piece at a time, with [PMTiles](https://github.com/protomaps/PMTiles), out of the one archive file Overture publishes each month. The newest release is looked up once a day |
| Detailed map picture | OpenStreetMap tile servers | [Tile usage policy](https://operations.osmfoundation.org/policies/tiles/) |
| Place details, speed limits | [Overpass API](https://overpass-api.de) (OpenStreetMap data) | One small question per place tapped; one per area for limits |
| Search, nearest address | [Photon](https://photon.komoot.io) (OpenStreetMap data) | |
| Routes | [OSRM](https://project-osrm.org) demo server (OpenStreetMap data) | |
| Weather, and for the sky: how much cloud in each of three layers, the wind, how far one can see | [Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com) | Asked at most every half hour |
| Live aircraft positions over Riyadh | [The OpenSky Network](https://opensky-network.org) | Its open feed, asked for one fixed box round the city (the same whoever asks, so nothing about you goes with the question), at most every 40 seconds, and far less often while it has nothing. It is fed by volunteers' receivers, of which there are few or none in Riyadh: most of the time it has no aircraft there |
| Where the stars are, how bright and what colour | The Hipparcos catalogue as extended by Anderson and Francis (XHIP, 2012) | Comes with the app (`js/stars.js`); nothing is asked of anyone. The moon and the planets are worked out on the phone |

These public services are run for light, fair use, which one person's app is.
They can be slow or briefly unavailable; the app then simply leaves that piece
of information out and carries on.

Not included, because no open source for it exists: live traffic. The moving
vehicles in the 3D city are a simulation on the real street network and say
nothing about the real traffic on a road.

**Buses and metro: the real hours and rhythm, not the real minute.** Riyadh
publishes when its buses and metro run and how often, but no timetable or
live positions as open data (the city's open data site that might have held
one is no longer online). So the app keeps to what is published:

- Metro: 05:30 to midnight, from 10:00 on Fridays; a train every four minutes
  in the rush hours (06:30 to 09:30 and 16:00 to 20:00) and every seven and a
  half minutes otherwise. Every line is given the same rhythm.
- Buses: 05:00 to midnight, about one every ten minutes each way along a
  street. Bus routes are not in the map data, only the stops, so a bus follows
  the streets that have stops and is taken off the road when it strays from
  them.
- A given end of a line sends its trains at the same minutes past the hour
  every time you look, so the picture is steady, but those are not the minutes
  of the real trains. Do not catch a train by it.

The map is only as complete as its sources are. Speed limits in particular
are missing on many roads. Bus stops are shown where OpenStreetMap has them,
which in Riyadh is many but not all.

Street lamps and the night. No open source says where a lamp post stands or
which lamp is lit. The lamps are stood by rule along the streets the map has
(every 30 to 42 m by the kind of road, 7 to 12 m tall), and their light takes
the colour usual for that kind of road in Riyadh. Which windows are lit, the
colour of a tower's band of light and the colour of Kingdom Centre's arch
(the real one changes from night to night) are set out by rule or by chance,
the same every night.

Buildings. Their outlines are real: recorded by mappers, or traced from
satellite photos by machine, which is why a few are a little off. What the
outlines do not say is made up to look right:

- Height. Landmark towers have theirs on record. Other buildings get the usual
  height for their kind (a house about 7.5 m), varied a little from one to the
  next, unless their shadow in the photo shows them to be taller.
- Windows. How many windows a wall has, and where, is not known. They are
  set out by rule: a floor to every 3.2 to 3.7 m of height and a bay to every
  2 to 4 m of wall, by the kind of building, the same for a given building
  every time. Which kind a building is goes by what the data says it is for
  and by its height: a low building is taken for a house, one of 60 m and
  more for a glass tower, one the data calls commercial is given shops along
  its foot. Blinds, the rooms that are lit by night and the colours of the
  signboards are picked by chance. None of this is what the real front looks
  like.
- Wall colour. Worked out from the colour of the building's own roof in the
  satellite photo: the roof's hue, much calmed, at the brightness of a wall in
  daylight. A guess that is right in kind more often than not (a sand-coloured
  district comes out sand-coloured), and wrong for any building whose walls
  are not the colour of its roof. Where the photo is switched off or shows
  too little, a building gets one of seven pale shades of plaster, picked by
  its number in the map data. A recorded colour is always used as it is.
- Roofs. Every roof is drawn flat, inside a parapet 60 cm tall and 30 cm
  thick: real for nearly every roof in Riyadh, wrong for the few that are
  pitched or domed. Its colour is the one the satellite photo shows where the
  sun is on the roof, calmed and evened out; for a building over 40 m, whose
  roof leans away from its outline in the photo, and wherever the photo is
  switched off or shows too little, it is one of seven usual shades, picked
  by the building's number. What a roof is covered with goes by rule (slabs
  on a low building, gravel on one of 28 m and more, sheet on works and
  stores). What stands on it is made up: placed by chance, the same way
  every time for the same building. None of it is what is really up there.
- A tower's special shape is not known, only its outline and height (the
  landmarks below are the exception). A building recorded in parts is drawn
  as its parts, and where the parts do not cover all of it the rest is bare
  ground.

Landmarks. Eight of Riyadh's are modelled by hand, as stacks of thin slabs
that follow the building's shape: Kingdom Centre, Al Faisaliah Tower, the PIF
Tower, Burj Rafal, Al Majdoul Tower, the TV Tower, the water tower and the
KAFD metro station. Position, outline and height are the real ones; the shape
was worked out from photos on Wikimedia Commons and from what is published
about each building, and simplified: a model, not a survey. The water tower
was built from memory, with no photo to check it against. The other tall
buildings of the city (the towers of the financial district among them)
remain plain blocks.

Bridges. Which stretches of road and track are on a bridge, and which pass
over which, is real. How high a bridge is, is not recorded: a road deck is
taken to be 6.5 m up and a metro viaduct 8.5 m, each level above another
5.5 m more, with a ramp down to the ground at either end, and a pier every
30 m. The ground itself is flat in this model, so a bridge over a valley, as
at Wadi Laban, stands no higher than a flyover. The Wadi Laban Bridge's masts
and cables are set out from a photo and from its published span of 405 m.

Metro stations. Where a station building and its entrances are, is real. The
golden screen, the vaulted roof, the footbridges and the pavilions are the
typical Riyadh Metro look, the same for every station; only the KAFD station
has its own model. A footbridge runs straight from the station to an entrance
across the road, which is about right but not surveyed.

Chain stores. Which branch is where comes from the map's places; the colours
are each chain's own, most of them looked up with
[Brandfetch](https://brandfetch.com) and a few set from a photo or from
memory, so one or two may be a shade off. What a branch really looks like is
not known: every branch of a chain gets the same simple dress, and a chain
the app does not know stays a plain building. No logo, name or sign of any
brand is drawn.

Two kinds of outline are left out on purpose: what the data marks as
underground, and any building a street runs straight through. The second rule
is for metro stations under a street, which are often on record as ordinary
buildings and would otherwise stand in the middle of the road.

If Overture's archive cannot be reached, the city is drawn with the street
map's own buildings instead. Those weld neighbouring houses into whole blocks,
have no roof furniture and are not measured by their shadows.

Heights read from shadows are rough, and mostly on the low side:

- A shadow can only be measured where it ends on open ground. In a dense
  street it lands on the building next door, and all that can be said is "at
  least this tall". A building that cannot be measured is taken to be about
  as tall as the measured ones around it.
- The time of day of the photo is not published, only the day. The app
  assumes the usual pass of a photo satellite, an hour and a half before or
  after midday, and picks whichever fits the shadows.
- A recorded height is never overruled, and no building is made lower than
  the usual for its kind.

Trees are found by colour, so a dark green roof or awning can be mistaken for
one and a tree in deep shade can be missed. Anything green on a road is taken
to be something else, so a real tree on a narrow traffic island can be missed
too. The photo cannot tell a palm from another tree. Which of the two shapes
a tree is drawn as goes by the size of its crown and by chance (a small crown
is more often a palm), the same every time for the same tree.

Shops. Where a shop is comes from the map's places. Its front is put on the
wall of its building towards the nearest street, which is usually right; the
awning says what kind of shop it is, and the colour of the sign is picked by
chance. What the shop really looks like is not known.

Mosques. Which buildings are mosques is real: those the data calls one, and
those with a Muslim place of prayer from the map inside them. Where a given
mosque's dome and minaret stand, and how tall the minaret is, is not known:
the dome is put in the middle of the roof, the minaret at a corner picked by
chance, and both are sized to the building.

The ground. It is drawn from what OpenStreetMap has, and where that has
nothing the ground is bare: the colour of sand, which in Riyadh is mostly
right. A lawn, a pool or a paved square is there if somebody mapped it. Not
drawn at all, because no source has them: garden walls, gates, what is parked
or stored in a yard, the paving of a forecourt.

The photo. In the Satellite look, how sharp it is depends on what the service
has for a place: about 30 cm to a pixel in Riyadh and other large cities,
coarser in small towns and open country, where the map shows the sharpest
level there is. By a rough count a sharp photo is some tens of megabytes of
download for an hour's driving, where the coarser one is a few; the same
streets driven again within a day come from the phone's own store. In the 3D
city the photo is read at one middling level only, which is a few megabytes
for an hour's driving, and nothing at all with the reading switched off.

Pavements. Hardly any pavement is in the map data. They are laid by rule: the
same width along every street of a kind (2.6 m beside a side street, up to
4.5 m beside a main road), none along a motorway or a service lane. Where a
street really has no pavement, or a wider one, the map does not say.

Road markings. The lanes painted are the ones the simulated traffic keeps to,
which go by the class of the road, not by a count of its real lanes. Whether
a given junction really has a zebra crossing is not known.

Parked cars are set out by chance along residential streets, the same way
every time for the same street. They are not the cars really parked there.
The same goes for car parks. Their lanes are on the map; the bays beside
them are laid by rule (2.7 m wide, 5 m deep, on both sides of a lane
wherever no building, road, pavement or tree is in the way), and about six
in ten are filled, three in ten beside a service lane that is not marked as
a car park's, and beside such a lane only within 45 m of a building: nobody
parks along a track through open ground. A real car park may have its bays
elsewhere, at an angle, or under a roof.

The sunlight is worked out on the phone from the date, the time and the place;
nothing is downloaded for it. Shadows are those of flat-roofed blocks on level
ground, and clouds are not taken into account. On the ground they are drawn
as exact outlines. On walls, roofs, cars and trees they are read from a
picture of the buildings as the sun sees them, in which a spot is about 80 cm
of ground: fine enough for the shadow of a building, too coarse for that of a
parapet or a lamp post, and a little wavy at the edge. Only buildings throw
these shadows, not trees or cars. The shade of a parapet on its own roof, and
of what stands on a roof, is worked out apart, as exact patches on that roof.
The landmarks' own models, the chain stores in their dress and the metro
stations are drawn by the map itself and take no shadow from their
neighbours.

## Put it online (free, from a phone)

The app is plain files, so GitHub Pages can host it.

1. Open this repository on github.com in the phone's browser.
2. **Settings → Pages**.
3. Under **Build and deployment**, set **Source** to *Deploy from a branch*,
   pick the branch that holds these files and the folder `/ (root)`, then
   **Save**.
4. After a minute the page shows the address, like
   `https://YOUR-NAME.github.io/REPOSITORY/`.

GitHub Pages is free for public repositories. Anyone with the address can open
the app, but it holds nothing personal: your position, settings and recent
links stay on your own phone. Your position is sent only as part of the
questions above (the weather, speed limits and addresses near you).

## Or put it online with Firebase

Firebase Hosting works as well, and can be done from a phone with Google Cloud
Shell, a command line in the browser that is already signed in to your Google
account.

1. In the [Firebase console](https://console.firebase.google.com), create a
   **new project** for this app and note its Project ID. Do not use a project
   that already serves a website: publishing would replace that website.
2. Open [Cloud Shell](https://shell.cloud.google.com/?show=terminal) with the
   same Google account, and allow it to use your account if it asks.
3. Paste this line, put your Project ID in place of `YOUR-PROJECT-ID`, and
   press Enter:

   ```bash
   rm -rf video-map && git clone --depth 1 -b claude/peaceful-tesla-y76kia https://github.com/Asms323/video-map.git && bash video-map/tools/firebase-deploy.sh YOUR-PROJECT-ID
   ```

4. When it says "Done", open `https://YOUR-PROJECT-ID.web.app` in Safari.

To bring a newer version online later, paste the same line again. The script
checks the address first: it publishes to an empty project or updates Video
Map, and stops and asks before replacing any other website.

## Using it in the car

This is a phone app. It does **not** appear as an app on the CarPlay screen:
Apple only allows that for approved apps built with a paid developer account,
and does not allow video and a map together in one CarPlay app.

What works:

- **Phone in a holder.** Turn it sideways for video and map side by side.
- **A screen-mirroring app.** If you use an app that mirrors the iPhone screen
  to the car display, it shows this app like anything else on the phone.
- **AirPlay.** Some new cars can show video from the phone on the car display
  while parked.

Watch video only when parked, or let a passenger watch.

## Limits

- A link has to be YouTube or point straight at a video file or stream. Pages
  from other sites, and films from services such as Netflix, cannot be played.
- Some YouTube videos do not allow playing outside youtube.com.
- A video picked from the phone has to be chosen again the next time the app
  opens; web apps are not allowed to remember files.
- Speed and direction come from the phone's GPS. Indoors, or standing still,
  the speed shows a dash.
- The route is an overview, not navigation: it does not follow you or re-plan.
- The map covers Riyadh only. Other cities are not drawn, and cannot be
  searched.
- The night is a picture of how Riyadh looks after dark, not a record of it:
  which rooms are lit, where each lamp post stands and what colour a tower's
  lights are is set out by rule, the same every night.
- The sky: the sun, the moon, the stars and the planets are where they
  really are (the moon and the planets to within a degree or so). The clouds
  are as many as the weather service says there are in each layer, but which
  cloud is where is made up: the service tells how much of the sky is
  covered, not the shape of it. With the weather switched off, the sky is
  clear.
- The aircraft: no free source that a web page may read has the aircraft
  over Riyadh. The open networks of receivers (adsb.lol, adsb.fi) have no
  receivers there and do not let a web page ask them; airplanes.live turns
  such questions away; the OpenSky Network may be asked, and is, but seldom
  has anything over the city. So what flies is nearly always the airport's
  usual traffic: the real number of flights for the hour (296,800 flights in
  2025, by the General Authority for Statistics), the real runways, the real
  wind and approach. Those aircraft are typical, not particular: no flight
  number, and not the minute any real flight lands. How the day's flights
  are spread over its hours is an estimate (the airport publishes no count
  by the hour): quiet before dawn, fullest in the evening.

## For developers

No build step. The folder is the site.

```
index.html              the page
css/app.css             styles
js/app.js               the app: layout, video, location, search, place details
js/map.js               the map: styles, markers, following, taps
js/life.js              the simulated traffic: street network, vehicles, trains,
                        and the bridges they cross
js/lights.js            the lights of the night: street lamps and their pools of
                        light, headlights, the light from shop windows
js/scene.js             the layer that draws into the 3D scene: the walls, the
                        roofs, the traffic, and the picture the shadows are
                        read from
js/walls.js             the walls: their shape, their measure and their colours
js/shade.js             the shadows on walls and roofs: what throws them
js/landmarks.js         the landmarks modelled by hand, as stacks of slabs
js/dress.js             metro station footbridges and pavilions, chain stores,
                        shop fronts, mosques
js/roofs.js             the roofs: cut along the buildings' outlines, their
                        colours and coverings, the shade of their parapets
js/sight.js             reading the satellite photo: shadows to heights, trees,
                        the colours of roofs
js/services.js          the open data services
js/lib.js               everything that needs no browser: parsing, distances,
                        road matching, place categories, map styles by day
                        and by night, the sun's position and the shadows it
                        casts, where Riyadh is
sw.js                   offline start
manifest.webmanifest    Home Screen name and icons
icons/                  made by tools/make_icons.py
firebase.json           what Firebase Hosting publishes, and how it is cached
tools/firebase-deploy.sh  publishes to Firebase, refusing to replace another site
vendor/maplibre/        MapLibre GL JS 5.24.1 and the right-to-left text add-on
vendor/pmtiles/         PMTiles 4.5.0, which reads the buildings archive
vendor/earcut/          Earcut 3.2.4, which cuts a roof's outline into triangles
tests/                  unit tests, browser test, small local server
```

Run it locally (location needs `localhost` or HTTPS):

```bash
node tests/serve.mjs
```

Unit tests, with Node 22 or newer:

```bash
npm test
```

Browser test, which needs Playwright with Chromium installed:

```bash
node tests/e2e.mjs
```

The browser test needs no internet. The street map is replayed from
`tests/fixtures/map.har`, a recording of the real OpenFreeMap service around
the test route in Riyadh, and the buildings from `tests/fixtures/buildings.json`,
the pieces of Overture's archive that route needs; the other services are
answered by stand-ins in the test file. To refresh both recordings from the
live services:

```bash
RECORD=1 node tests/e2e.mjs
```

The test covers Chromium only; check changes on a real iPhone in Safari too.

When you change the app, raise `VERSION` in both `js/app.js` and `sw.js` so
phones drop their saved copy.
